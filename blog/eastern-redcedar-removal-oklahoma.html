Eastern Redcedar Has a Place

Eastern redcedar belongs in Oklahoma. The harder question is whether it belongs everywhere it grows.

August 18, 2026 Christian Castillo Oklahoma

Eastern redcedar (Juniperus virginiana) is one of the most familiar trees in Oklahoma. It grows along fence lines and rocky slopes, beneath mature hardwoods, across pastures, beside houses, and seemingly anywhere else a bird can carry a seed.

And it belongs here.

Eastern redcedar is native. Its dense foliage provides winter cover for wildlife, its berry-like cones feed birds and other animals, and a mature tree in the right place can be a beautiful part of the landscape.

So the case for cedar removal should not begin with the idea that cedar itself is bad.

A better question is: What is this piece of land supposed to be?

One cedar is different from a hundred

A well-placed cedar can be an asset. A dense stand gradually filling open ground is something different.

Eastern redcedar readily establishes in Oklahoma grasslands, pastures, woodland edges, fence lines, and disturbed ground. Historically, recurring fire helped limit young cedars in many of these environments.

When fire disappears from the landscape, young cedars survive long enough to mature. More seed arrives. More seedlings establish. Scattered trees can slowly become continuous evergreen cover.

At that point, the important management question is no longer whether each individual cedar is healthy. It is whether the direction of the entire plant community matches what the landowner wants from the property.

Selective removal can make a woodland better

Some of the most useful cedar removal does not look like land clearing at all.

A mature oak may be surrounded by younger cedars. A woodland edge may have become so dense that little light reaches the ground. A trail may have disappeared. An area behind a house may simply have become too thick to see, walk through, or maintain.

Removing selected cedars can open growing space around trees worth preserving and allow more light to reach the understory.

That may benefit grasses, forbs, shrubs, tree seedlings, and other vegetation—but cutting alone does not guarantee ecological recovery. What comes next still depends on soil, seed sources, browsing, invasive plants, moisture, and future management.

Removal is an action. Restoration is a direction.

Around a home, the calculation changes

Residential cedar can be useful. Dense evergreen foliage makes a good visual screen and provides winter structure when deciduous trees are bare.

The same tree can become less useful when it grows immediately beside a house, driveway, shed, fence, utility, or commonly used part of the property.

Dense cedar can obscure sightlines and make areas difficult to inspect or maintain. Fine evergreen foliage and dead material within the crown also deserve consideration when evaluating vegetation near structures.

That does not mean every cedar beside a house is dangerous. It means vegetation around a home should be intentional.

Sometimes selective removal produces a surprisingly simple benefit: a forgotten part of the property becomes usable again.

You can see through it. You can walk through it. You can maintain it. And once the cedar is opened up, you can often finally see what else is growing there.

Oklahoma field research

Cedar can also create favorable tick habitat

This is a practical concern that comes up repeatedly on Oklahoma properties, especially where people, pets, livestock, or workers regularly move through dense vegetation.

Ticks are vulnerable to drying out. Beneath eastern redcedar, dense evergreen foliage changes the environment near the ground. Shade, accumulated litter, and greater humidity can create a more favorable refuge than nearby exposed grass.

Oklahoma researchers have measured that difference directly.

96.4%

In a 2023 field study at seven sites across central and western Oklahoma, researchers placed paired CO₂ traps beneath individual eastern redcedars and in nearby grass patches. Of 3,654 ticks collected, 3,522 — 96.4 percent — were collected beneath the cedars. Only 132, or 3.6 percent, were collected in the paired grass locations.

Lone star ticks accounted for nearly all of the ticks collected. Among lone star ticks specifically, 98.2 percent of nymphs and 91.9 percent of adults were collected beneath eastern redcedar. All 16 American dog ticks collected in the study were also found beneath cedar.

The researchers suggest that the microenvironment beneath cedar helps explain the difference. Leaf litter and relatively high humidity can give ticks protection from desiccation, while wildlife using cedar for food and cover may also contribute to where ticks become concentrated.

This does not mean that an Oklahoma property with cedar has 27 times as many ticks overall. The study measured where ticks were captured within paired cedar-and-grass sampling locations. Nor does removing cedar make a property tick-free. Wildlife hosts, surrounding vegetation, weather, moisture, and season all matter.

What the research does show is that even individual eastern redcedars within an encroached grassland can provide substantially more favorable tick habitat than the open grass immediately around them.

Small cedars are much easier to manage

Eastern redcedar gives landowners one major advantage: young trees are comparatively easy to remove.

A cedar small enough to cut with a hand saw is a very different job from the same tree after another twenty years of growth.

This matters most on acreage. One seedling is almost inconsequential. Hundreds of seedlings are the beginning of a different landscape.

Periodic inspection and selective removal can accomplish with hand tools and chainsaws what years of neglect may eventually turn into a substantial clearing operation.

Fire is part of the story

Eastern redcedar expansion in Oklahoma is closely tied to the loss of recurring fire.

Many Oklahoma grasslands and open woodlands developed with fire as a normal ecological process. Young cedar was suppressed while grasses and other fire-adapted plants persisted.

Modern land is different. Homes, roads, fences, subdivisions, fragmented ownership, and changes in land use all limit where and how fire can safely occur.

Prescribed fire remains an important land-management tool, but it requires proper planning, conditions, expertise, and coordination. It is not realistic for every property.

Mechanical cedar removal therefore fills an important role. A saw does not reproduce everything fire does, but it can prevent every opening from gradually becoming dense woody cover.

Dense cedar also changes fire behavior

There is another side to the fire relationship.

Mature eastern redcedars hold dense evergreen foliage and often retain dead interior material. When many trees grow together, that creates continuous woody fuel.

Around homes and structures, density and arrangement matter more than the simple presence of one cedar.

A single healthy tree in an appropriate location is not equivalent to a dense wall of evergreen vegetation running toward a house.

The right amount of cedar depends on the land

There is no useful universal rule for how many eastern redcedars a property should contain.

Around a home, access, visibility, structures, screening, fire fuels, frequently used areas, and future mature size may be the most important considerations.

In a woodland, the question may be which trees should form the future canopy. A young cedar beneath a mature oak may be less important to the future stand than the oak above it.

On pasture or open acreage, the question becomes broader. Is the land supposed to remain pasture, prairie, savanna, wildlife habitat, woodland, or some intentional combination?

And sometimes the right answer is simply to keep the cedar.

A healthy mature eastern redcedar in a good location may provide privacy, wildlife habitat, winter structure, and beauty for decades.

Don't clear just for the sake of clearing

Once someone learns how readily eastern redcedar can spread, it is easy to begin treating every cedar as a problem.

That is the opposite mistake.

Good stewardship requires discrimination rather than hostility toward a species.

  • Keep trees that contribute to the landscape.
  • Remove trees working against the purpose of the site.
  • Protect desirable mature trees.
  • Maintain openings where openings are valuable.
  • Preserve cover where cover is valuable.
  • Consider what should occupy the space after removal.

The goal isn't removal. The goal is a better landscape.

Some of the most satisfying land-management work begins in places that do not look remarkable.

A crowded woodland edge. An abandoned corner behind a house. A fence line disappearing into vegetation. A pasture slowly filling with cedar. A mature hardwood surrounded by younger trees competing for the same space.

Selective removal can reveal the structure that was already there.

A trail reappears. Sun reaches the ground. Air moves through the understory. A mature tree has room around it. The property becomes easier to see and easier to use.

Instead of an undifferentiated mass of vegetation, individual trees and spaces begin to have purpose.

Eastern redcedar has a place. The work is figuring out where that place is, and where something else would serve the land better.
