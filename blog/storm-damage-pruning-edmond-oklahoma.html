What You Find in the Crown

A storm-damaged mature tree became a lesson in observation: when to cut, when to preserve, and why the best plan is allowed to change once the tree gives you better information.

August 24, 2026 · Christian Castillo

The objective

Correct storm tearout, inspect affected structure, and improve the immediate landscape around a mature tree the homeowner wanted to retain.

The constraint

Perform necessary summer work without allowing a localized problem, or the convenience of already being on site, to justify unnecessary pruning.

The result

Compromised crown structure was corrected, most functional canopy remained, and existing stone and soil were repurposed elsewhere within the property.

A ground inspection can give an arborist a plan. The tree gives him the rest of the information.

This mature tree in Edmond had weathered more than one episode of storm damage. Broken limbs and older wounds were visible within the crown, but the tree still carried a substantial amount of healthy, functioning structure.

The homeowner wanted the storm damage corrected. The timing also made practical sense: other woody debris was already being removed from the property, so the pruning debris could be consolidated into the same haul-off.

That was a good logistical reason to perform the work. It was not a good reason to prune more tree than necessary.

Before climbing, I gave myself a simple working hypothesis: address the damaged material, inspect questionable attachments, retain healthy structure wherever reasonable, and keep the overall pruning dose conservative.

Then I went up to see whether the tree agreed.

The storm had already changed the crown

Storm-damage pruning begins from a different starting point than routine pruning.

A storm has already removed branches, created wounds, and changed the distribution of the crown. The result may look irregular, especially from the ground.

That irregularity can tempt a person to continue cutting until the tree looks visually balanced again.

But a mature tree is not a geometric object.

The objective was to correct the damage, not to make healthy portions of the tree match it.

Sound branches and healthy foliage still have value. If one part of the crown has already been lost, removing healthy material elsewhere simply to create symmetry can turn a limited injury into much broader canopy loss.

So every additional cut needed to answer a simple question:

What does removing this actually accomplish?

A hypothesis, not a prescription

From the ground, my framework was straightforward: correct torn and broken material, inspect damaged attachments, prefer smaller interventions where they accomplish the objective, and leave useful structure alone when there is no compelling reason to remove it.

But mature trees contain history that cannot always be read from below.

Branch unions overlap. Foliage hides wounds. Cavities can be almost invisible from one direction and obvious from another.

Once I reached one of the damaged portions of the crown, the difference between planning and seeing became very clear.

From within the crown, an old cavity associated with the damaged structure became much easier to understand. It was important information, but not a verdict on every branch around it.

What looked simple from below was more complicated up close

The attachment contained a substantial old cavity that was not fully legible from the ground.

Finding a cavity matters, but the existence of decay or missing internal wood does not automatically dictate removal of an entire limb or tree. Mature trees often continue living with old injuries, cavities, callus growth, and other structural imperfections.

What mattered here was context.

I could now inspect the relationship between the cavity, the affected portion, and the live structure around it. I could view the union from several directions instead of trying to infer everything from the lawn.

The original hypothesis still helped.

But now the tree was providing better evidence.

Let the tree change the plan

This is where principles become useful, or dangerous.

A good principle should improve judgment. It should not replace it.

Remove what needs to come off. Preserve what can responsibly remain.

As I moved through the crown, some planned cuts became unnecessary. Other defects deserved closer attention than they had from the ground.

The presence of one problem did not become permission to keep expanding the pruning around it.

After each meaningful intervention, I reassessed the remaining tree.

The question became less: What else could I prune while I am here?

And more: What problem would the next cut solve?

When there was no convincing answer, the branch stayed.

A small win with a larger lesson

My favorite cut of the day

One corrective cut became my favorite result from the entire job.

It was not the largest cut. It was not especially dramatic.

I liked it because it represented a boundary.

The cut addressed what needed correction without allowing that discovery to become justification for progressively removing more healthy tree.

The pile on the ground is not the product. The condition of the tree when we leave is the product.

Good pruning is a series of decisions

From the ground, tree work can look mostly physical: climb, position, cut, move material, repeat.

The physical side is real, but mature-tree pruning also involves a continuous stream of decisions.

Remove or retain? Reduce or leave? Is this defect important enough to justify another wound? What changes elsewhere in the crown if this branch comes out? Does the next cut improve anything?

Eventually, one question becomes especially important:

Have I already accomplished the objective?

There is a point in good pruning where the correct next cut is no cut at all.

Back on the ground

The work did not end in the crown

The area around the base of the tree had its own longstanding landscape problem: a deteriorating ring of stone enclosed the root-zone area immediately around the trunk.

Rather than rebuild the same feature, we dismantled it.

The stone was not discarded. It was moved elsewhere on the property and reused to construct a new landscape feature.

A dilapidated raised bed in the backyard was also being removed. Its existing soil was reused to reshape disturbed grade around the tree and create a simpler transition into the surrounding lawn.

No new decorative material was necessary.

Good stewardship is often less about adding something new than making better use of what is already there.

Two kinds of restraint

Looking back at the finished job, the work in the crown and the work on the ground shared the same underlying idea.

In the canopy, restraint meant not removing healthy branches simply because the saw and climbing system were already in the tree.

On the ground, restraint meant not treating every landscape problem as an excuse to purchase and install something new.

Existing stone found a new use. Existing soil found a new use. Healthy tree structure stayed in the tree.

The interventions were different, but the logic was similar:

Keep what is useful. Correct what is not. Add only what the site actually needs.

The finished property

The tree still looks like the same mature tree

That is part of the success.

Corrective pruning does not always create a dramatic difference in crown size, and it should not have to.

The damaged and compromised portions were addressed, but the mature canopy the homeowner wanted to keep remained largely intact. At ground level, removing the deteriorating stone enclosure created a much more visible change to the property while allowing the existing materials to remain useful elsewhere.

For the property owner

Good tree work can be difficult to measure by what is missing

A mature tree represents decades of accumulated growth. When part of that tree is damaged, the purpose of corrective pruning is not to demonstrate effort by producing the largest possible brush pile.

It is to identify what requires intervention, preserve the healthy structure that still contributes to the tree, and leave the property better than it was found.

On this job, the most satisfying result was not simply what came out.

It was what remained: a substantial mature crown, a cleaner relationship between the tree and the surrounding landscape, and site materials that continued serving the property rather than becoming waste.

The tree gets the final word

Before this job, I wrote down the principles I expected to use.

That turned out to be useful.

The hypothesis shaped what I looked for and gave me a reason to resist unnecessary pruning. But it never became more important than what I actually found in the tree.

Once in the crown, the cavity became clearer. Branch relationships changed with perspective. Some possible cuts proved unnecessary. Other details became more relevant.

So the plan changed.

That is not a failure of planning.

It is the purpose of observation.

Good tree care requires standards, experience, and principles. It also requires enough humility to let an individual tree complicate them.

I started with a hypothesis.

Then I climbed and looked.

The cuts followed the evidence.

The materials on the ground found better uses.

And when the tree and the property had received what they needed, we stopped.
