Follow up

Before estimating

Know what you're selling

Better information produces better estimates, stronger customer confidence, and cleaner production.

Customer

Who owns or controls the property? Who makes the final decision? What outcome matters most to them?

Scope

Which trees or areas are included? Removal, pruning, cleanup, hauling, stump work, landscaping, or another service?

Site

Check access, gates, fences, structures, utilities, traffic, neighboring property, terrain, drop zones, and equipment limitations.

Timing

Is this urgent, flexible, seasonal, tied to an event, or simply exploratory future work?

Risk

Look for compromised trees, difficult rigging, valuable targets, decay, utility proximity, poor access, and specialized equipment needs.

Expectations

What does “finished” mean to this customer? Clarify cleanup, haul-off, wood handling, surface protection, and exclusions.

Selling well

Sell the result, not just the labor

Do not apologize for a good price.

The customer is buying judgment, risk management, skilled labor, equipment, insurance, cleanup, property protection, communication, and a completed result.

Price should support excellent execution. A job that cannot be performed properly at the sold price was not sold well.

When presenting work:

  • Explain what you observed.
  • Explain what you recommend.
  • Explain what the crew will actually do.
  • State the price clearly.
  • Stop talking long enough for the customer to respond.
  • Answer the real concern behind objections.
  • Ask for the work.

Before reducing price

Find the real objection.

“That's expensive” does not always mean the customer needs a lower price.

They may need more confidence, clearer scope, better timing, a smaller project, or simply time to decide.

Ask yourself:

  • Do they understand what is included?
  • Do they trust us?
  • Is timing the real issue?
  • Can scope change instead of price?
  • Is the customer comparing the same scope?
  • Am I discounting because they need it, or because I am uncomfortable?

Follow-up

Stay useful without becoming noise

Follow-up exists to help the customer make a decision, not to pressure them into one.

After estimate

Make sure the customer received everything and invite questions.

2–3 days

Check for unanswered concerns, scope questions, or changes in timing.

About 1 week

Reconnect politely and ask where the project stands.

Longer-term

Keep worthwhile opportunities alive when timing, budget, or circumstances change.
